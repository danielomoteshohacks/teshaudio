Most of what we’re told about safe listening comes down to one number. Noise standards such as NIOSH and OSHA express exposure as a single A-weighted level: measure the sound in dB(A), note how long it lasted, and compare the result with a limit. It is simple, and simple is useful. But the longer I spent with the physics of hearing, the less that single number seemed to describe what is actually happening inside the ear.

## The weighting is least accurate when it matters most

A-weighting is a filter applied before the level is measured, and it approximates the ear’s response at low loudness, around 40 phon. That is a reasonable model of a quiet room. But at the high levels where damage happens, the ear’s response flattens. So the weighting is least accurate exactly when it matters most: it describes a quiet moment well and a loud session poorly.

## The cochlea is not one sensor

The bigger problem is that a single number treats the ear as one sensor. It isn’t one. The cochlea is tonotopic: different places along the basilar membrane respond to different frequencies. It is closer to a row of frequency-specific sensors than to a single microphone.

And those sensors are damaged unevenly. One region can wear out while the others are still fine, and one overall level cannot tell you which region is taking the load. Two sessions with the same A-weighted reading can put very different stress on different parts of the same ear.

## Why the region around 4 kHz goes first

Part of the reason the damage is uneven is the ear canal itself. Its resonance boosts roughly the 3 to 4 kHz region by about 10 to 15 dB. That is part of why noise-induced hearing loss typically shows up as a notch around 4 kHz. The region the ear amplifies most is the region that tends to fail first.

## The reflex that doesn’t save you

The ear does have a defence, the acoustic reflex, but it offers limited protection. It is slow, taking tens of milliseconds or more to respond. It acts mostly below about 2 kHz, which leaves the region around 4 kHz largely to itself. And it fatigues under sustained exposure, which is exactly the kind of exposure a long session produces.

## Why producers should care

Producers are a high-risk group, and not because we are careless. Our sessions are long. Headphones couple energy straight into the ear canal, right where that resonance sits. And the work itself pushes us louder: we turn up to check whether the vocal has enough presence, or whether the sibilance is under control. Each of those checks pushes more energy toward the part of the hearing range that is already most exposed.

A meter that reports one A-weighted number can say the session was fine while one part of the cochlea quietly took most of the load. That is the gap I wanted to close.

## What I built instead

That gap is why I built Beyond the Decibel. Instead of collapsing everything into one number, it follows the structure of the ear.

First, a mix goes through a gammatone filterbank, which splits the signal into bands in a way that models the frequency analysis along the basilar membrane. Second, for each band it tracks cumulative exposure over time, so the load that builds up in one region isn’t averaged away by the others. Third, it turns that picture into advice specific to the mix in front of you: when to switch from headphones to monitors, how much to turn down, and when to take a break.

It is a research and prediction tool, not a medical device, and it isn’t a substitute for having your hearing checked. What it gives a producer is a view that follows how hearing actually works, so the decisions made in a session aren’t based on a number designed for a quieter world.

Your ears are the one piece of gear you can’t replace. They deserve more than a single number.
