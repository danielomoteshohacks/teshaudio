I spend a lot of my life with headphones on. Making beats in FL Studio, arranging in Cubase, listening back to a mix for the hundredth time to check one snare. If you make music, or you just love it, you probably know this feeling: you take your headphones off after a long session and the room sounds a little dull. Sometimes there’s a faint ring that wasn’t there before. By morning it’s gone, so you forget about it.

For a long time I thought hearing safety was simple: check how loud it is, stay under the number, and you’re fine. Then I started reading about how the ear works, and it blew my mind. It’s one of the most detailed, delicate parts of the human body, and one number on a meter tells you very little about what’s going on inside it.

None of this is my discovery. Scientists have understood most of it for decades. It just mostly sits in textbooks and research papers, a long way from the people who need it: listeners, producers, DJs, church instrumentalists, students with earbuds in all day, anyone who loves sound.

This is my attempt to explain it in plain language.

## First, what is sound?

Sound is just air moving. When a drum is hit, the skin pushes the air next to it, that air pushes the air next to it, and a wave of tiny pressure changes travels outward, like ripples spreading on water when you drop a stone in.

Those ripples reach your head hundreds or thousands of times every second. How fast they wiggle is the pitch (the frequency, measured in hertz, Hz). How big the wiggles are is the loudness. A deep bass note might wiggle 50 times a second. A cymbal shimmer might wiggle 10,000 times a second.

Your ear catches those wiggles and turns them into signals your brain can read, in three stages.

## Stage one: the outer ear

<SoundJourney />

The outer ear is the part you can see, plus the canal that leads inside. It collects sound and funnels it toward your eardrum.

Your ear canal isn’t a neutral pipe. It’s a short tube, about two and a half centimetres long, and like any tube, it resonates. You know how blowing across the top of a bottle makes a note? Your ear canal does something similar. It naturally boosts sounds roughly between 2,000 and 5,000 Hz, by up to about 15 to 20 dB by the time they reach the eardrum.

That’s useful, because a lot of speech clarity lives in that range: the “s”, “t” and “k” sounds that help you tell words apart. It also means sound in that range reaches your inner ear hotter than the rest, which comes up again further down.

## Stage two: the middle ear

Behind your eardrum are the three smallest bones in your body: the malleus, the incus and the stapes. Together they’re smaller than a grain of rice.

When the eardrum vibrates, these bones carry the vibration to the inner ear and concentrate it on the way. The eardrum is fairly large, and the opening into the inner ear is tiny, so the force gets focused onto a small spot, the same way a stiletto heel puts more pressure on the floor than a flat shoe. That’s how the ear gets airborne sound into the fluid inside your inner ear without losing most of it.

There are also two small muscles in the middle ear that tighten when sound gets loud, reducing how much energy gets through. It’s a bit like squinting in bright sunlight. People sometimes call this the ear’s built-in protection, but it has limits. It mostly reduces low frequencies, not the upper mids where so much damage happens. It takes a moment to react, so it can’t catch a sudden bang. And like your eyes squinting all afternoon, it gets tired during long exposure.

## Stage three: the inner ear, where hearing really happens

Deep inside your skull is the cochlea, a fluid-filled tube coiled up like a snail shell. If you could unroll it, it would be about 3.5 cm long. Running along its length is a strip called the basilar membrane, and it’s stiff and narrow at the start, then wider and floppier at the far end.

Because of that, different pitches make different spots vibrate. High sounds shake the stiff start. Low sounds travel further and shake the floppy end. Everything in between has its own place.

The closest picture I have is a piano keyboard rolled up into a spiral, with every key in its own spot. The cochlea sorts every sound you hear by pitch before your brain gets involved.

When I learned this, I kept thinking about how deliberate it all is: the shape of the canal, the tiny bones, the rolled-up keyboard. I don’t believe that level of detail is an accident. I believe we were made to experience sound deeply, and that makes looking after it matter even more.

## The tiny cells that do all the work

Along that keyboard sit the hair cells.

Each ear has only about 15,000 of them. Roughly 3,500 are inner hair cells, which turn vibration into the nerve signals your brain reads. The other 12,000 or so are outer hair cells, which act like tiny amplifiers, physically pumping to make quiet sounds louder and sharpen your sense of pitch.

On top of each hair cell is a little bundle of microscopic “hairs” called stereocilia. When sound moves the fluid, the bundles bend, and the cell fires.

Picture a field of grass. Walk across it once and it springs back up. Send a crowd stampeding across it every day and eventually the grass stops standing up at all. Loud sound does something similar to these bundles. Too much, for too long, and they get bent, broken, or the whole cell dies.

## So what is a decibel, really?

We measure loudness in decibels (dB). Decibels don’t behave like normal numbers, which is where a lot of people get misled.

The scale is logarithmic, a bit like the one used for earthquakes, so a small change in the number is a big change in the sound:

- Every 3 dB more means double the sound energy reaching your ear.
- Every 10 dB more sounds roughly twice as loud to most people, but carries ten times the energy.

So 94 dB isn’t “just a bit more” than 85 dB. It carries about eight times the energy.

Most safety numbers are also “A-weighted”, written dBA. A-weighting is a filter that turns down the low frequencies before measuring, to roughly match how sensitive our ears are at quieter levels. It’s a sensible compromise, used everywhere from factories to phone apps, but it still squeezes a whole spectrum of sound into one number.

## What one number misses

A sound meter gives you one number. Your ears respond to a lot more than that, in at least five ways.

### 1. Where the energy sits

Two sounds can show the exact same dBA reading and still hit your cochlea differently. Because the ear canal boosts the upper mids, energy around 2,000 to 5,000 Hz arrives at the inner ear stronger than a meter in the room suggests.

Think of it like two bags with the same total weight. One is packed evenly. The other has everything heavy stuffed on one side. The scale shows the same number, but the strap takes very different strain.

This is part of why noise damage often shows up first around 3,000 to 6,000 Hz. On a hearing test, it can appear as a dip in that region, often called the “4 kHz notch”, although not everyone shows it.

### 2. How long you listen

What matters is how loud and for how long, taken together. Scientists call this the dose.

I like to think of it like a data plan. The widely used workplace guideline from NIOSH (the US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) gives you a daily “bundle”: 85 dBA for 8 hours. Every 3 dB louder, your bundle burns twice as fast. At 88 dBA you get 4 hours. At 94, 1 hour. At 100 dBA, which is a typical concert or club, your whole day’s bundle is gone in about 15 minutes.

Unlike a data plan, though, you can’t top it back up.

### 3. The shape of the sound

Steady noise, like a fan or an engine hum, and sharp, punchy sound, like hammering or gunshots, can have the same average level but not the same effect. Research on workers exposed to impulsive noise suggests the usual rules, which mostly look at average energy, can underestimate the risk from sounds with sudden peaks.

Music sits somewhere in between. Heavily compressed tracks are more like steady noise. Live drums, with their sharp kicks and snares, carry real peaks. The average alone misses that.

### 4. Damage the usual test doesn’t see

This one surprised me the most.

In 2009, two researchers, Sharon Kujawa and Charles Liberman, exposed mice to noise that caused only temporary hearing loss. A few weeks later, the mice’s hearing tests looked normal again. But when the researchers looked closely, up to about half of the connections between the inner hair cells and the hearing nerve were permanently gone.

It’s like a phone whose screen still lights up perfectly while some of the wires behind it have been cut. Everything looks fine until you try something demanding, like following a conversation in a noisy room.

This is called cochlear synaptopathy, sometimes “hidden hearing loss”, because a standard hearing test can come back normal while real damage has happened. Scientists are still debating how much this happens in humans and how best to measure it. But it’s a strong reason not to treat “my hearing came back” as “no harm done”.

### 5. You

People are different, too. Genetics, age, health, and how much loud sound you’ve already been through all change how vulnerable your ears are. Two friends can stand side by side at the same concert and walk out with different results. A safety limit is built around an average, and nobody is exactly average.

## Some things we tell ourselves

**“If it doesn’t hurt, it’s fine.”** Pain only kicks in at extremely high levels, well above the point where damage starts. Your ears can be getting hurt long before anything feels painful.

**“My hearing came back, so I’m good.”** Muffled hearing and ringing that fade overnight are a warning, not a reset. As the mouse study showed, some damage can stay even after your hearing seems normal again.

**“I’m young, I’ll worry about it later.”** Hearing damage adds up quietly over the years. The habits you have in your teens and twenties are what decide how your ears sound in your forties and fifties.

## The warning signs

Your ears do give warnings. If sound seems muffled after something loud, or you hear ringing, hissing or buzzing (tinnitus), your hearing has been pushed past what it can comfortably handle. It often fades within hours or a day, which is why people ignore it.

Treat it like the low-fuel light in a car. And if ringing or muffled hearing lasts more than a day or two, please see a doctor or an audiologist.

## How much is too much?

These are the guidelines you’ll see most often:

| Guideline | What it says |
|---|---|
| NIOSH (workplace) | 85 dBA for 8 hours; safe time halves every 3 dB |
| OSHA (US legal limit) | 90 dBA for 8 hours; safe time halves every 5 dB |
| WHO–ITU (personal listening) | About 80 dB for up to 40 hours a week for adults (75 dB for children) |
| WHO (venues and events) | Average of no more than 100 dB over any 15 minutes |

For a sense of scale: normal conversation is around 60 dB. Busy city traffic can reach around 80 to 85 dB. Concerts and clubs commonly sit around 100 dB or more. And many phones with earbuds can go past 100 dB at full volume, right inside your ear.

## Why this matters now

The World Health Organization estimates that more than 1.5 billion people live with some degree of hearing loss. It also estimates that more than 1 billion young people aged 12 to 35 are at risk because of how loudly and how long they listen to music and other sounds.

WHO also says around half of all hearing loss can be prevented.

In Lagos, loud is normal: generators humming all night, traffic and horns, church sound systems, Owambe parties, buses with the music turned all the way up. Nobody thinks of it as a risk, because it’s just life here, but your ears are taking all of it in.

Hearing gives you music, but also a friend’s laugh, your mum’s voice on the phone and the sound of rain on the roof. Most people agree that losing your sight would be one of the hardest things in the world. We rarely say the same about losing our hearing, and we should.

## How to protect your ears (without giving up the music)

You don’t have to stop enjoying loud music. A few habits go a long way:

- **Turn it down a little.** Even a few dB makes a big difference to your daily dose. If someone next to you can hear your earbuds clearly, it’s probably too loud.
- **Use your phone’s hearing features.** Most phones now have a headphone volume limit or a sound safety warning. Turn it on.
- **Use noise-cancelling headphones if you can.** Blocking outside noise means you don’t have to crank the music up just to hear it over a bus or a generator.
- **Take breaks.** Give your ears some quiet time, especially after anything loud. Silence is how they recover.
- **Wear earplugs at loud events.** High-fidelity earplugs lower the level evenly, so the music still sounds clear, only quieter.
- **Step away from the speakers.** At a party, a concert or church, distance is free protection.
- **Get a hearing test,** even if you think your hearing is perfect. A baseline helps you notice changes early.

## For my fellow music people

If you make music, you work with your ears. Every mixing decision, EQ move and call on whether a song feels right depends on them.

Mix at a moderate level most of the time and only check loud briefly. Take regular breaks in long sessions, because tired ears make worse decisions anyway. That’s when the hi-hats creep up and the mix sounds harsh tomorrow. And protect your ears at gigs, rehearsals and services, not just in the studio. The drummer next to you is louder than you think.

## The one piece of gear you can’t replace

You can buy new headphones, new monitors, new plugins, a whole new studio. You can’t buy new hair cells.

I’m not writing this to make anyone scared of sound. I love sound, and knowing how the ear works is how I plan to keep enjoying it for the rest of my life.

<ClosingLine>So turn it down a little. Your ears are the one piece of gear you can’t replace.</ClosingLine>
